guest_intro: "Dr. Regina Druz is an integrative cardiologist, physician executive and digital health entrepreneur. A cardiac imager by training, she completed her cardiology fellowship in 2001 and trained at Cornell and Duke before building an integrative, prevention-focused practice. She has led teams of up to 30 people, run a cardiac stress testing section at a large hospital, and served as chief of cardiology in an underserved inner-city area. She is passionate about health optimization and longevity, builds practices and platforms, and is now developing AI tools intended to change how medicine is practiced. She is a mother of three and writes the Substack Vital Signs and Bottom Lines."
editor_name: "Hiba Hamdar, MD"
editorial: "I am still in residency, so I asked this question as someone who wants to know what is waiting for me. I asked Dr. Druz to skip the general story of women in medicine and tell me what was different for her, in her own specialty. She answered with honesty: the beeper she handed to her attending on the way to labor and delivery, the years when working part-time was held against a woman, the leadership roles where she was seen as a clinician first, and a patient crying because her children were about to come off the bus. Her answers move from the personal to the scientific, from the underrepresentation of women in cardiovascular trials to how technology could finally close that gap, and she gives both an encouraging and an honest version of her advice to a young woman considering cardiology. She ended with three short messages, for women, for mothers, and for leaders in medicine. I hope they stay with you as they have stayed with me."
qa:
  - q: "To start, could you introduce yourself to our audience?"
    a: "Thank you for inviting me. I'm Regina Druz. I'm an integrative cardiologist and an AI entrepreneur, looking to build things that will hopefully change how medicine is practiced, by me, for me and for my patients."
  - q: "Cardiology has historically been one of the more male-dominated specialties. Beyond the general narrative, what were the specific challenges you faced entering and staying in this field?"
    a: "Cardiology has come a long way. When I trained, there was a desire to bring more women into medicine, especially into traditionally male-dominated specialties, but the desire didn't have a structure behind it. I finished my fellowship in 2001, and the year is now 2026, so a lot has changed in 25 years. There are definitely more women in cardiology now, and even women in leadership roles, which is fantastic. But as a journey, it was very tough, because I chose a fast-paced specialty that deals with critically ill patients who need immediate care, and I also chose to have a family, to be a wife and a mother.\n\nI'll give you an anecdote. When I was pregnant with my first child, my son, I was finishing my first year of cardiology fellowship. Part of fellowship is being on call, so when someone comes in with an acute heart attack, the cardiology team comes down, and fellows are a big part of that. We have to see the patient, make sure they're okay, call our attending physicians and get them quickly to the cath lab, because there's no time to wait. I was at the end of my pregnancy, maybe three or four weeks left, and I was so tired. It was my last call, and after that I would have had maternity leave. I went home and thought, wouldn't it be great if I went into labor, and instead of being on call, I could just do what I was ready to do and give birth to my son. And that's exactly what happened. My water broke, and since it was my first child, labor was a little slow. The next morning I went upstairs to the coronary care unit, and my attending looked shocked because I showed up in my civilian clothes. I said, \"Here's your beeper. I'm giving it to you because I'm going three floors up to labor and delivery.\"\n\nThink about how crazy it is that a woman ready to give birth to her first child is partly relieved because she's so tired and doesn't have the energy to be on call for the next three days. In intense, procedural specialties like cardiology, pulmonology, gastroenterology and surgery, I think we've made a lot of progress, but we should be turning that progress into a daily reality for the women who choose to specialize in them."
  - q: "How hard is it to manage time as a mother who is also a doctor?"
    a: "Very hard. I'm a mother of three, and I'm very proud of my children, who are young adults now. When they were younger, I always asked myself whether I was doing the right thing, whether I should spend more time going to their school activities. I went to most of them, though of course I missed some. You always have the pressure of having to be somewhere else.\n\nMost of my early career, when my children were young, was a while back. Working part-time wasn't really a thing, and as a woman physician you were expected to work full-time just like your male counterparts. Telemedicine wasn't a thing either, so there was no real flexibility. The few women physicians who chose to work part-time often had it held against them, interpreted as \"she's not into it\" or \"she doesn't care enough to be here full-time on this important mission.\" Twenty years ago people would say this. Now, the good thing is that we no longer live in an era where a woman or a man working part-time is a deviation, and people can do it the way that works for them.\n\nBut that was the ethos of medicine, and I'm not sure we've fully left it behind. In many places there is still a belief that if you've had all this education and training, your mission is to work full-time in service of your colleagues and patients, and the human part doesn't get discussed. Even television shows, like Grey's Anatomy, which is very popular here in the United States, show main characters with no work-life balance. Their life is their work. We want to make a difference, but at the same time we perpetuate the ethos that your life is your work, which shouldn't be true for any human being."
  - q: "You've held executive roles leading teams of up to 30 people while practicing clinically. Did you face different obstacles moving into physician leadership as a woman, compared with staying purely clinical?"
    a: "It's a great question, and I don't know that there's a good answer. What I find in general, and this could be true not only for women but even for men, is that when you're a clinician trying to move into a leadership position, especially a business leadership position, the leaders in the C-suite who aren't clinical will continue to see you as a clinician first and foremost. They want to lean on your clinical skill set and give your business skill set or strategic mindset a second seat, while you want to do the opposite.\n\nSo I think physicians should consider additional education and certification, whether it's a business degree, some other training or executive coaching. I think every physician who wants to go into leadership should consider this at some point in their career. But it's tough, because there is always that perception that you're a clinician first."
  - q: "What does a female cardiologist bring to patient care that is additive, something specific to the experience of being a woman in this role?"
    a: "There was a study some time ago, not super recent, showing that patients often prefer female cardiologists to their male counterparts, because we tend to be a little more nurturing and approachable in how we speak with people, and we take time to explain things. I honestly think it's not fair to stereotype, because there are many men who do exactly the same thing, and women who don't. But in general, that's what the studies show: patients feel that women spend a little more time and explain things a little more.\n\nPart of it comes from the fact that in cardiology and several other specialties, the illness and the acuteness of the situation is very difficult for a patient. Patients need more than your clinical acumen. They need your emotional support and your leadership. They need to know you're in their corner. How we communicate that culturally is a little different between men and women, although each individual can figure it out for themselves.\n\nAnd as you get older, patients who used to assume you were a nurse eventually recognize that you're a doctor. I've worked with some fantastic nurses in my lifetime, but 20-some years ago, a patient seeing a woman in a white coat would automatically assume she was a nurse, and they'd have to reorient themselves to the thought that she might actually be their physician. That's still going on, to a degree."
  - q: "Was there a patient case that shifted how you see your role as a woman in cardiology, or as a doctor in general?"
    a: "It wasn't a very dramatic case, but it shifted my thinking. I was running a section on cardiac stress testing at a large hospital, the flagship institution of what is now a very large healthcare system. One of our patients was a fairly young woman who had to have a stress test. She didn't have a primary care doctor, so she came to the ER with chest pain. It was low-risk chest pain, so the protocol was a stress test, and if she passed, she'd go home. My nurse called me and said I needed to speak with this patient. She was crying and very upset. She hadn't realized that coming to the emergency room would be a whole-day affair, and her children were coming home from school in about an hour. She was supposed to be there to get them off the bus, and she had no one to help her. That created a lot of stress and anxiety for her.\n\nIt got me to reflect on the whole arc of what has been happening with women in cardiology, not only as physicians but also as patients. For many reasons, including childbearing risks and caretaking responsibilities, women were excluded from clinical trials for most of the history of cardiology. It's only recently, within maybe the past 20 to 30 years, certainly over the course of my 25-year journey, that including women in cardiovascular trials became the norm, even mandatory. Some trials include fewer, some more, but the National Institutes of Health looks at gender and ethnicity because they want different groups represented, so that the information we get isn't specific to just one group. That changed my perception, because until then I had thought everybody gets the same thing, but they don't. That's why many women cardiologists focus on women's cardiovascular health, because of this misrepresentation over many, many years, and other things in research that didn't quite work out for women. We now have an opportunity to rectify that with the new generation."
  - q: "What would you change in research, academia and clinical practice, for example to include women who are underrepresented in cardiovascular trials?"
    a: "I think we need to bridge the gap. Going back to my patient who was crying and didn't have the luxury of time to wait for a stress test because her kids were coming off the bus and she had no plan B, that lack of access to clinical trials, or even to the most contemporary treatments, is where the issue still is. It isn't only for women, but also for people who don't have the means, for minorities, and for people who live in underserved areas. I worked in such an area as a chief of cardiology, an inner-city area where, whether men or women, people had very limited access to resources.\n\nRight now we're in an era where technology is at an inflection point, and that has opened up access. If we leverage technology smartly, we could provide access to clinical trials, population investigations and registries, or even give people an opportunity to submit real-world data.\n\nTake menopausal hormone therapy, which I often deal with as an integrative cardiologist. The research trials done to date didn't give us good answers. In fact, they gave us tainted answers that, for many generations of women, closed the door on hormone therapy, a door that is now opening. But we still don't have a good way of knowing what works and what doesn't. None of the studies to date has shown an advantage with regard to cardiovascular outcomes, so there's still a big gap in knowing what women of a certain age should be doing, and yet women receive this therapy every single day. With the FDA removing the black box warning from hormone preparations for cardiovascular disease, significantly more women are asking for these treatments and getting them. We even have a shortage of estradiol patches. That's a perfect opportunity to leverage technology to gather outcomes from these women, at a fraction of the cost of randomized clinical trials, so that we get the answers we need, and it could benefit thousands or hundreds of thousands of women. For new physicians like you, still in residency, this is an exciting opportunity to use technology to drive improvement in people's lives."
  - q: "If a young woman today told you she wanted to go into cardiology, what would you tell her: the encouraging version and the honest version?"
    a: "The encouraging version is that after 25 years of practice, if I were to make my choice again, I would choose cardiology again. I love being a cardiologist. It's a specialty that allowed me to grow not only as a physician but as a person. There are so many things we're able to do right now, with cutting-edge imaging, genetic investigations and an understanding of biomarkers, and cardiology is also one of the specialties that provides evidence for longevity medicine, which I love doing.\n\nThe honest version is that before you do this, think of your family, and think about whether your family will benefit from it. If your family is supportive and you have help, that counts for a lot. If you don't have a supportive family, or don't foresee yourself having help, and you feel you'll be in a position where you have to make a very hard choice between being there for your kids or being there for your cardiology patients, that's not a choice you should ignore. Think it through now, so you can make the best decision for yourself, your family, and your future patients, because they want all of you. They don't want you interrupted by phone calls from your kids' school, and that will happen, because no matter how much help you have, that's life. If you're going to be in an intense period with young children and full-time cardiology responsibilities, that may not be a sustainable path. So go into it clearheaded.\n\nI was fortunate to have phenomenal role models, women cardiologists I met along the way, who had full-fledged careers and wonderful families, including children. It wasn't easy, but they showed that it's possible. Still, the cost is personal to each of us, and everybody has to be aware of it. In the past, you wouldn't even be asked. It would just be assumed that you'd somehow manage your family while being a full-time cardiologist. Now we can actually talk about it, and hopefully, at least in training, there are opportunities for women to explore these more demanding specialties without sacrificing their families. I think it's happening, but not fast enough."
  - q: "You trained at Cornell and Duke in conventional cardiology before building an integrative practice. What did conventional training miss, and how have colleagues responded?"
    a: "Traditional cardiology is very focused on interventions, and interventions obviously save lives. When someone comes in having a heart attack, or needs a pacemaker, a defibrillator or a new valve, these are extraordinary procedures that have changed people's lives. But the problem is that you then miss everything that led to the event. The person arriving in the emergency room in their 50s or 60s with a first heart attack is someone we ideally would have had as a patient for the past 20 or 30 years, starting in their 30s, working preventively to reverse some of the root causes and understand comprehensively what their risk factors are. That's what integrative cardiology does. At the end of the day, it's comprehensive cardiology. We don't only ask what procedure or what drug a patient needs. We ask why they need a procedure or a drug, what got them there, and what they can do to start reversing those predispositions.\n\nWhether you call it integrative medicine or longevity medicine, which are all a little different, although it gets fuzzy, it gives patients back their agency. Patients who want to take control and own their health find a good partner in an integrative practitioner. Many traditional cardiologists are my partners, and I refer to them. But what I very commonly hear from patients is that they're not getting the explanation or the partnership. They're getting a transaction: you have high cholesterol, here's your prescription. They may still end up with the same prescription, but that's only the beginning of a much longer journey that looks at the other factors shaping their health and cardiovascular trajectory. It's the difference between a final destination and the beginning of a journey."
  - q: "How do you see AI changing cardiology, from reading EKGs to imaging to predicting risk, and where do you want the human cardiologist firmly in charge?"
    a: "I was recently at an executive summit on AI and healthcare, where the CEO of a company in the AI space said something that resonated with me. We often talk about the human in the loop when it comes to AI, he said, but we should be talking about AI in the loop of human care. That's the reversal. AI is a phenomenal tool, but it's only a tool. In my opinion, cardiologists will continue to be the primary drivers of cardiovascular health, partners with their patients, using AI tools as needed to expand their care and practice at the very top of their license, to get visibility and assistance they might not otherwise have. It's not delegating care to AI. It's using AI to augment the care you provide.\n\nGoing back to women being underrepresented in clinical trials, what a fantastic opportunity there is in using decentralized trial design and AI tools to give women who are busy taking care of children and families an opportunity to be recruited into trials. AI's ability to look at massive data sets and derive insights we would never have figured out otherwise is remarkable, like reading a million-plus ECGs and finding patterns that predict the onset of heart failure within five to ten years. I'm a cardiac imager by training, and I'm fascinated by a technology called FAI, the fat attenuation index. It's AI-enabled and measures the degree of inflammation in the fat surrounding the coronary arteries, which we know contributes to arterial inflammation and atherosclerosis. A recent study was able to derive these parameters from non-contrast CT scans, the ones done for a calcium score, which are cheap, quick and accessible to everyone. That opens doors to the integrative, comprehensive path I've been walking for the past five-plus years. When a patient has a calcium score of zero but shows a lot of perivascular fat inflammation, you need to ask what they should do about lifestyle, exercise, stress and sleep, and yes, maybe give them or increase a medication, but there's so much more you'll have to be doing. I'm pleased to see these developments, and I think we are the leaders of AI, not the followers."
  - q: "Some residents now rely on AI answers without checking what is behind them. How should medical education respond?"
    a: "That's scary, and it's also my concern. My kids graduated from college within the past few years, and I asked them how AI will change higher education, because what used to take hours or days can now be done in minutes. I think education, especially medical education, will have to redefine itself, and it could be as simple as going back to some of the old techniques. When I was in medical school, we had oral examinations in addition to written ones. We had to go and examine a real patient without using any books, present our findings, and be graded. The skill set of putting things together, working through a differential diagnosis, and forming a rapport with a patient so you get the right information transcends AI. I hope residents don't lose that, because then they wouldn't really be physicians. They'd just be button-clickers."
  - q: "Could you leave us with one message as a woman in medicine, one as a mother in medicine, and one as a leader in medicine?"
    a: "As a woman in medicine, I can say: you've got this. As a mother in medicine, I'm going to say: they see you. That's your family, and they see your effort. I did ask my children at one point how they felt that I wasn't always there, and they said they knew I worked hard, and I could see that they learned from that example. So remember that they see you. And as a leader in medicine, which is probably the hardest one for women to master, and I'm still working on it myself: trust yourself."
